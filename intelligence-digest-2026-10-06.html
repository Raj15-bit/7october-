How to read the map

The world tiles are mostly red. Borrowing costs and fuel prices are at or near records. The India economy tiles are mostly green, because car sales, tax collections and factory activity are all strong.

The India market tiles sit in between. Foreign investors sold Indian shares in each of the last 7 sessions, even though the economy is doing well. So Indian shares are being priced on what the world charges for money and fuel, not on what Indian shoppers are spending. That gap is the main story of this issue.

A higher yield on a government bond lifts the cost of every other loan, from a home loan to a loan for a data centre. That is why the same event appears again in the AI section (04) and the Indian banks section (05).

Shares and bond yields are rising together

One view is that shares and yields rising together is healthy. US services activity was the strongest in 5 years, and growth in the 3rd quarter looks near 4%.

The opposite view is that the pair rising together is a warning. Only 21.4% of US shares are above their 50-day average, and a Bank of America framework says yields above 6% start to hurt share returns.

Both views can be true for a while. Shares can keep rising on strong profits until yields reach a level where lenders prefer bonds, and nobody can name that level in advance. Section 03 shows the evidence for each side.

How the forces connect

The chain below runs from the war to the Indian share market. Each step makes the next one worse. Nothing in it needs a new shock; the first step is enough.

STEP 1War near Hormuz keeps ships and pipelines under attack
STEP 2Tanker hire and diesel hit records, so fuel costs rise
STEP 3Prices rise, so lenders ask for more on long bonds
STEP 4Dear money hits AI debt, new listings and costly shares
STEP 5Foreign investors sell, and Indian shares fall despite a strong economy

The chain is why a fight around a narrow sea channel ends up in an Indian investor's portfolio. Dear fuel pushes prices up. Central banks answer with higher rates, or lenders ask for them. Dear money then hits the most borrowed-up and most expensive parts of the market first: AI data centres, new listings and shares with high prices against profits.

India sits at both ends of the chain. It imports most of its oil, so fuel costs hit its airlines, its farmers and its budget. It also has strong home demand, which is why the economy tiles on the map stay green.

02

Oil, Hormuz and the shipping squeeze

Barrels are moving again, but ships, diesel and physical oil cost far more than the screen price.

In short
  • Tankers are still being hit in and near the Strait of Hormuz, and nobody claims the attacks.
  • The screen price of oil is near $100, but oil bought for real delivery costs over 20% more.
  • Diesel and ship hire hit records, so freight and food costs are the next bill to arrive.

Ships are still being hit

4 tankers were struck from 1 to 4 October on the Omani side of the strait, and another was hit on 5 October. In every case the weapon was described as an unknown projectile and no group took responsibility.

  • Kazimah III — a Kuwaiti crude carrier; 5 Indian crew were taken off the ship.
  • 3 October — the UK ship-safety centre (UKMTO) issued a late report of a crude tanker struck above the waterline inside the strait.
  • 5 October — a tanker's master reported a strike by an unknown projectile that started a fire in the engine room; no casualties were reported at the time.
  • Totals — 91 vessel damage incidents since the war began in late February, and a UKMTO warning in force until mid-October.

Are the flows back to normal?

One view is that Hormuz traffic has recovered.

The head of the trader Vitol said about 14 million barrels a day crossed the strait over 7 to 10 days.

Shell's chief executive put Middle East flows at more than 80% of pre-war levels.

The opposite view is that the recovery is thinner than it looks. Oil for real delivery (Dated Brent) trades at $127 while the futures price is near $103.

Ships turn off their tracking signals, so counts differ: the US counted about 32 passages a day over 72 hours to 4 October, while an independent tracker saw single digits.

A strange pattern in options also fits this: buyers of protection against higher Brent prices are active while sellers of protection are quiet, which Goldman Sachs called a dislocation.

The Saudi bypass pipeline keeps being hit

The East-West pipeline carries Saudi crude about 745 miles to Yanbu on the Red Sea, so it avoids the strait. It was reported attacked again on 4 October, at Pumping Station No. 2 at Khurais, and later reports said flows resumed normally. Aramco's chief executive said Brent would have reached up to $200 without it.

One view is that the pipeline is the weak point of the whole system, and it will be offline more than online over the next 6 months because the Houthis can keep hitting it. The opposite view is that it keeps recovering within days: the energy minister's 5.8 million barrels a day is above the 2.65 million that ship tracker Kpler saw after the September restart. Both views use the same facts, so loading data at Yanbu is the thing to watch.

Ships now cost more than the oil on board

Hiring a very large crude carrier (a VLCC, a ship that carries about 2 million barrels) from the Gulf to China now costs about $1.3 million a day.

The Wall Street Journal reported that Gulf producers pay up to $40 million for a tanker "shuttle run" through the strait, and sailors are offered up to $25,000 a trip.

Diesel has its own war

US diesel peaked at a record $6.53 a gallon on 21 September and was $6.32 on 5 October. It was $3.76 in late February, before the war. Diesel is the fuel of trucks, farms and factories, so its price spreads through the whole economy.

Governments are responding in the same way they did for petrol, with stock releases and tax relief. Each step is a short-term patch.

  • US tax order — an order on 5 October lets farmers and truckers delay diesel tax payments to 31 December without penalty, and lets red-dyed (off-road, tax-free) diesel be used on highways in that period.
  • European Union — 50 million barrels of diesel reserves to be released over 2 months, which France's president also proposed from the oil reserve.
  • United States — a further release of about 40 million barrels, which completes a 172 million barrel plan.
  • Strategic stocks — US reserves are 283 million barrels, the lowest since 1982, after 28 weekly falls; the IEA board meets on 14 and 15 October to set the details of the G7 diesel and oil release.

One view is that these releases buy time until flows recover. The opposite view is that the market has already moved from a surplus of oil to a deficit, that higher buying by China adds pressure, and that releases only slow the fall in stocks. The IEA meeting falls before the US midterm vote, which makes the timing look political to that second group.

16 trucking firms went bankrupt between late August and 21 September, so there are fewer trucks for the same cargo. Freight cost is part of every shelf price, so the rise is likely to reach grocery prices in the weeks ahead.

How long will this last?

One view is that the damage will take years to repair. An author put repairs to Gulf energy sites at about 5 years and up to $2.53 trillion, and said world diesel would be 3 million barrels a day short for that long. Diesel exports from the Gulf were down to a quarter of pre-war levels by August.

Aramco's chief executive said world stocks are "scarily thin" and could take 2 years to rebuild.

The opposite view is that the worst is over. President Donald Trump said petrol prices are now driven by Russian refineries that Ukraine has blown up, not by Hormuz. Crude has fallen from the highs, and the Saudi pipeline is pumping 5.8 million barrels a day.

A slow supply response favours the first view. The IEA says oil fields lose about 5.7 million barrels a day each year when nobody invests, against about 2.2 million in 1980. Big oil companies are returning cash to owners instead of growing output, so new supply is not coming fast.

Iran and the Saudi front

  • Iran's economy — seaborne crude exports were zero in September, the rial hit a record low near 2.7 million to the dollar, and official inflation is 89.8%.
  • Hormuz conditions — Iran's parliament repeated 7 conditions for reopening the strait, including lifting blockades and sanctions, and its army says it wants longer-range missiles.
  • Yemen — Houthi forces hit airports in Riyadh, Jizan, Najran and Abha and the Rabigh refinery, while the Saudi-led side says it destroyed 324 targets and government forces say they took the port of Mocha, which Houthi sources dispute.
  • Weather — a tropical disturbance has a 70% chance of developing; Louisiana refines about 3 million barrels a day, one-sixth of the US total.

The Gulf of Aden and the Red Sea matter as much as Hormuz, because the Houthis sit next to the Bab al-Mandab strait. The Houthis now hold ground near the Bab al-Mandab strait, so they have more power over shipping on that route. If both routes stay unsafe, tanker hire stays high.

The price of money: yields at 24-year highs

US government bonds hit their highest yields since 2002 while US shares sat close to a record.

In short
  • The cost of lending to the US government rose to the highest level in 24 years.
  • Japan, the UK and France saw the same pressure, and France was the weakest link.
  • Shares are still near a record, but fewer and fewer of them are going up.

The US 10-year yield reached 5.34% on 5 October, its highest since 2002.

The 30-year yield passed 5.70% for the first time since 2002.

The 20-year yield reached 5.75%.

The short end moved less than the long end.

The gap between 2-year and 10-year yields moved in under 2 weeks.

It went from 18 basis points to 48.

Analysts call this a bear steepener: long bonds fall faster than short bonds, which usually means lenders want extra pay for lending for a long time. Only 15 such episodes have happened since 1990.

The odds that the Federal Reserve raises rates again in October fell from 64% to 22% after a weak jobs report. Yields still stayed high, so the market is pricing in something other than the next rate move.

Why are yields rising?

One view is that lenders have lost trust in how the US pays its bills. Gross federal debt is $40.2 trillion, net interest costs about $1.0 trillion a year, and the Financial Times reported that the average maturity of US debt is only 2.6 years, so the government must refinance fast at the current higher rates.

The investment firm Bridgewater's founder, Ray Dalio, said a US debt crisis could come to a head within 3 years.

The opposite view is that the trust story is wrong and the cause is strong growth and dear fuel. The Institute for Supply Management's services price index reached 74.0, its highest since July 2022, and US services activity is the strongest in 5 years. A bond investor rejected the trust story outright when it was put forward.

Both views agree on 1 point: oil made it worse. Dear fuel raises inflation, inflation raises the chance of rate hikes, and rate hikes lift yields. That is the chain section 01 showed.

The US is not alone.

Japan's 30-year yield hit a record 4.12%.

Its 10-year yield reached the highest level since 1996.

Its 20-year yield reached a 31-year high.

Government borrowing costs in big countries are rising together, so there is no safe place to hide inside bonds.

France is the weak link in Europe

Deutsche Bank's Jim Reid wrote that the gap between French and German 10-year yields rose 32 basis points in a single week.

It reached 141, the biggest weekly widening in Bloomberg data since 1990.

It touched 160 basis points on Friday. A wider gap means investors want more pay to hold French debt than German debt.

  • Euro — fell to $1.1219, its weakest since May 2025, on the French budget worry.
  • Banque de France — its governor, Emmanuel Moulin, warned that risk is being "strangled by interest rates".
  • Relief on 6 October — French 10-year yields fell about 16 basis points and the gap to Germany narrowed 12 basis points after Marine Le Pen published a shadow budget with spending cuts.
  • European Central Bank — Deutsche Bank thinks its final rate will be 2.75% to 3%, below the 3.5% that markets priced 8 days earlier.

A calm day in France does not end the problem. Spain has also called a snap election for 29 November.

Fear in bonds has not made investors run away. They are buying bonds on the dip: money poured into the iShares 20+ year Treasury bond fund (TLT), and US municipal bond funds took in $4.2 billion in a week, a record since 2004.

One view is that this is smart money locking in the highest yield in 24 years.

A few giant companies are holding up the index. The 10 largest AI-linked shares are now 42% of the S&P 500.

Single-sector bubbles in the past peaked near 40% of the market, and railroads reached 63% in 1873. The chart above shows that breadth is usually weaker in the autumn, so a thin market in October is common even without a crash.

US consumers are worried

  • Spending on computers — US business spending on computers rose $20.5 billion in the 2nd quarter to a record $420.1 billion, nearly 3 times the level of late 2023.

The consumer is weaker than the companies. That is the gap that bond traders point to when they say the rate-hike case is weak and shares are strong only because of AI spending.

What would change the story

  • Lower yields — would need oil to fall for good, which needs Hormuz flows to be truly back (section 02).

  • Gold — the Bundesbank's president defended gold's role as debt risk crowds out bond yields.

The main point is simple: the price of money is set by oil, deficits and trust, all at once. Section 05 shows how this reaches Indian banks and shares.

The AI build-out: who pays, and for how long

The AI build-out is now a borrowing story, and the lenders are the ones carrying the risk.

In short
  • Big technology companies and AI labs are borrowing record sums to pay for chips and data centres.
  • Power, not chips, is now the slowest part of the build.
  • The most important question is whether AI revenue grows fast enough to repay the debt.

Why Chinese households spend so little

Chinese households saved a record amount.

One view is that the cause is confidence. Property prices have fallen for more than 5 years, and sales of old homes have passed sales of homes under construction for the first time.

The opposite view is that the cause is income and welfare. ThePrint's opinion piece points out that China's public health and social security spending is only about 3% of GDP each, against 7.2% and 8.2% for OECD countries, and that household savings exceed one-fifth of GDP, more than any OECD economy.
